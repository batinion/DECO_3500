# Literature

Secondary research behind the domain, **supporting relationships through nostalgia and reminiscence**. It was done in the first phase (weeks 5–7) and presented in the team pitch, before the interviews narrowed the concept. Later readings used in evaluation are listed at the end.

## Framing the domain

We split nostalgia into three pairs of branches to decide where to focus:

| Branch | The two sides |
|---|---|
| Personal vs collective | Memories of one person vs memories a group holds together |
| Restorative vs reflective | Wanting to get the past back vs thinking about it from the present |
| Social vs sensory | Triggered by people and conversation vs triggered by objects, smells, music |

Six reasons the domain matters came out of the reading and early interviews (ages 20–60+):

- **Recreative curation:** people recreate and rearrange memories rather than simply storing them.
- **Digital vs physical asymmetry:** physical mementos feel more significant than digital ones.
- **Deliberately unopened memories:** some memories are kept but never revisited.
- **Contested inheritance:** what gets passed down, and to whom, is not straightforward.
- **Place-triggered recall:** locations bring memories back.
- **Socially learned methods:** how people preserve memories is picked up from family and peers.

## Key findings

| Finding | Source | How we used it |
|---|---|---|
| Autobiographical remembering serves ongoing psychological needs | Bluck et al., 2005 | Reminiscence is worth supporting, not just a nice-to-have |
| People mainly preserve memories through external artefacts like photos, videos and messages | Wardell & Palombo, 2024 | Submission should accept the formats people already use |
| A memory lasts through emotional significance and relevance to identity | Wardell & Palombo, 2024 | Prompts ask about people and moments that mattered, not routine |
| Memories can stay accessible for decades | Hirst et al., 2015 | Supports a long sealing period (years, not weeks) |
| Digitally created memories tend to feel less significant and are forgotten more easily than physical mementos | Petrelli & Whittaker, 2010 | The digital capsule needs the feel of a deliberate object: the rocket |
| In intergenerational storytelling, listeners actively discover, decipher and reconstruct fragmented stories rather than passively receiving them | Jones & Ackerman, 2018 | The reveal should be reconstructed by the group (puzzle, then guessing game) |

## Concepts these led to

The pitch presented four research-backed directions:

- **Digital time capsule:** memories sealed and opened later. *(Chosen; became Space-Cap.)*
- **"What did it look like then?":** past views of a place.
- **Collective audio tree:** a group's shared voice memories.
- **Ancestral language preservation:** capturing fragmented elder knowledge.

## Readings used later in the project

| Reading | Used for |
|---|---|
| Olsson et al., 2020: review of technologies for collocated social interaction | Redesigning the reveal: engaging people in collective activity works better than raising awareness |
| Epstein et al., 2015: design framework for social sharing in personal informatics | Mapping the capsule's data, sharing trigger, persistence, content and audience |
