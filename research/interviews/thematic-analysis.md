# Thematic Analysis: Discovery Interviews

Eleven interviews were run; the ten here (P01–P10) were run in two rounds, affinity-mapped on the team Miro board and grouped into seven themes. Each theme below lists the supporting evidence and the design decision it led to. Participant details are in [`README.md`](README.md); full responses in [`participant-responses.md`](participant-responses.md).


## Round 1: first impressions (P01, P03, P04, P05)

Four people from very different backgrounds, asked open questions to see what habits exist before narrowing anything down.

- Everyone used a **mix of physical and digital**; nobody relied on one method.
- Life stage seemed to matter: the 60+ participant (P03) had a daily diary and a very organised system; the younger two were more casual and reactive.
- **Digital storage running out** was the moment people were forced to decide what to keep.
- **Happy and sad memories were treated differently**: sad ones skipped, minimised, or kept but never looked at.
- P05 stood out for avoiding text entirely and preferring voice recordings and photos.
- Motivation was mostly personal; P03 was the exception, building a photo collection for nieces and nephews.

These shaped the 14-question guide for round 2 and narrowed the theme to **who:** groups of friends or family, **when:** close to a farewell, with closure at a reunion, **what:** preserving memories, **why:** emotion, reminiscence, stronger bonds.


## Summary of themes

| # | Theme | Design implication |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | Medium is chosen to match the memory's emotional register, not habit alone | Accept photo, text and voice, not one format |
| T2 | Future retrieval vs in-the-moment processing | Make submitting itself meaningful; seal it for later |
| T3 | Curation is action-driven, not comprehensive | Low-effort drops over time; safe space for what people filter out |
| T4 | Physical preservation is deliberate; digital is passive, shaped by algorithms and storage | Give the digital capsule the weight of a deliberate object |
| T5 | Revisiting is rare and trigger-driven, almost never scheduled | Build the trigger in: a scheduled reunion and reveal |
| T6 | Audience sits on a self ↔ legacy spectrum | Design for the middle: a peer group |
| T7 | Scaffolding and prompts unlock reflection people don't do unprompted | Fill-in-the-blank memory prompts |

## T1. Medium is chosen to match the memory's emotional register, not habit alone

- **P02:** happy memories get photos; when sad and overwhelmed, she writes instead, then decorates the page with stickers.
- **P04:** keeps jewellery and clothing because of who gave them, not the item itself.
- **P05:** prefers voice notes and photos over writing; laminates important letters and photographs them as a backup.
- **P06:** keeps durable objects as they are; photographs anything likely to deteriorate.
- **P07:** photo by default, video only for movement or performance.
- **P08:** written letters are good but *"never the same"* as voice recordings.

**Implication:** one format won't fit everyone. Memory drops support photos, write-ups, songs and voice (Experience Requirement: *support multiple input formats*).

## T2. Future retrieval vs in-the-moment processing

For some, preserving *is* the point; they aren't writing for a future reader.

- **P02:** preserves memories for *"Nothing because I am preserving it in the moment"*; she has already worked out the lesson while writing, so rereading adds little.
- **P03:** describes his daily diary as an act of appreciation.
- **P08:** tries to feel a memory in the moment without dwelling on it.
- **P02:** often only realises something was meaningful *"at a much later time."*

**Implication:** submission should feel worthwhile on its own, and the value of looking back should come from distance. This pointed us toward a **sealed, delayed** capsule.

## T3. Curation is action-driven, not comprehensive

People rarely decide what to keep on purpose. Something forces it: running out of storage, a breakup, a move.

- **P04:** curates only when phone storage runs low; discards items tied to painful relationships.
- **P07:** keeps almost everything, with no real selection.
- **P01:** *"preserve it for as long as I can and once it's gone, it's gone."*
- **Good vs bad:** P06 explicitly excludes "triggering" memories; P05 keeps painful ones but never revisits them; P03 documented painful periods far more sparsely; P01 keeps some things to himself *"because either too depressing for them to hear."*

**Implication:** capture should be low-effort and spread over time, and sharing must feel safe. Drops are opt-in and anonymous, and nothing is visible until the reveal.

## T4. Physical preservation is deliberate; digital is passive, shaped by algorithms and storage

- **Physical, deliberate:** P03 scans and catalogues photos with a handwritten index; P01, P08 and P09 keep a dedicated box so things don't get lost; P10 presses flowers in books.
- **Digital, passive:** P06 relies on Facebook's "On this day"; P08 sees old photos through the Apple widget; P10 is prompted by a cloud "memory from one year ago"; P04's phone gallery is only touched when storage fills.
- Physical items were kept with more intention and visited more.

**Implication:** a digital capsule needs the weight of a deliberate object. The rocket gives the group one shared container they consciously load and launch together.

## T5. Revisiting is rare and trigger-driven, almost never scheduled

- **P01:** looks back *"Rarely, probably once every six months or a year"*, out of boredom.
- **P07:** family albums visited about once a year; old emails and Facebook photos essentially never.
- **Triggers:** conversations (P07, P09), music (P08), smells and objects (P10), anniversaries (P03).
- A few revisit more often: P03 (daily notes, anniversary rereads), P07 (around twice a week via conversation).

**Implication:** if no one returns unprompted, the product has to be the trigger. Space-Cap sends a reunion notification and the group reveals the capsule together.

## T6. Audience sits on a self ↔ legacy spectrum

- **Self:** P06 keeps everything *"just for me"*; P04 and P07 preserve mainly for themselves.
- **Legacy:** P03 builds a photo catalogue for nieces and nephews; P07 values inherited family albums.
- **Values over things:** P05 would pass on lessons, not objects.
- **In between, peers:** P09 sends old photos to restart conversations with friends he rarely sees; P10's old friends use shared memories as common ground; P08 preserves to cherish friendships.

**Implication:** we designed for the middle of the spectrum: a **group of friends** who will drift apart (international students finishing a course), not the individual and not future generations.

## T7. Scaffolding and prompts unlock reflection people don't do unprompted

- **P05:** realised during the interview itself that she values people and lessons over objects more than she thought.
- **P03:** rereading an old diary surfaced details he had forgotten.
- **P02:** recognises meaning only when looking back at photos later.
- Several answers were "not directly addressed" until a specific question was asked: reflection depended on being prompted.

**Implication:** an empty text box isn't enough. Memory Stars use pre-authored fill-in-the-blank prompts (e.g. *"I never told ___ this but ___"*) to help people say something they wouldn't otherwise.

## Rumsfeld matrix

| | |
|---|---|
| **Known knowns** (consistent across interviews) | Preservation is hybrid, matched to how the memory feels. People revisit rarely and only when triggered. Positive memories are preserved better than negative ones. Digital storage limits force decisions; physical doesn't. |
| **Known unknowns** | Whether people would commit to "seal it, open it years later", the assumption the concept rests on. How long people want to keep memories (never asked directly). |
| **Unknown knowns** (tacit behaviour) | People don't realise they're sorting memories by importance until storage pressure forces it. Some trust a platform (e.g. Facebook) to keep things forever without questioning it. |
| **Unknown unknowns** | What happens to memories if the platform or account is lost. Whether the group will actually show up years later: willingness now ≠ follow-through then. |


## From interviews to requirements

The themes became six experience requirements:

| Experience requirement | Comes from |
|---|---|
| Helps people recall memories in a group context (prompted Memory Stars, free text for flexibility, minimum number of stars) | T7, T3 |
| Helps people log memories within a period in a group context | T2, T3 |
| Keeps friends and family in touch over a long period (capsule in space for a set number of years) | T5, T6 |
| Helps the group reminisce and strengthen bonds (collaborative activity everyone takes part in) | T6 |
| Enables cyclical reunions rather than a one-time reveal | T5 |
| Supports multiple input formats (photo, text, voice) | T1 |


We also mapped the concept against Epstein et al.'s (2015) design dimensions for sharing personal informatics data (data domain, sharing trigger, persistence, post content, audience).

## How this connects to user testing

Interviews told us how people relate to memories. User testing ([`../../user-testing/`](../../user-testing/)) checked whether the prototype actually gets a group sharing and revealing them together.

| Interview theme | Design decision | What testing showed |
|---|---|---|
| T5 Rare, triggered revisiting | Scheduled group reveal | Tutor: reveal felt passive; redesigned into an active guessing game and shared-control space game |
| T6 Peers in the middle | Group-only capsule; every member contributes | A/B test: shared controls made most sense, so everyone has a role |
| T7 Prompts unlock reflection | Fill-in-the-blank prompts | Prompts reused in the flash-card reveal (guess who a memory is about) |
| T1 Emotional register | Photo, text, voice formats | Puzzle reveal broadened beyond photos |
| T3 Filtering / safety | Anonymous, opt-in drops | Anonymity kept so guessing during the reveal stays fun |
